Why is the first yellow milk called “liquid gold”?

What is breastfeeding?

Breastfeeding is feeding an infant with breast milk directly from the mother’s breast.

Birth → 6 monthsExclusive breastfeeding

  • Breast milk is the only food and drink.
  • Recommended for the first 6 months of life.
  • No water, juice or other foods are routinely needed.

From 6 monthsAfter 6 months

  • Continue breastfeeding.
  • Start complementary foods.
ExclusiveBreastfeeding + complementary foods
Birth6 m12 m2 years +
Clinical pearl

Breast milk is about 88% water, so a baby under 6 months who is exclusively breastfed needs no extra water, even in hot weather. Prescribed medicines, vitamin or mineral drops and ORS are allowed.

Benefits of breastfeeding

6 benefitsFor the infant

  • Provides ideal nutrition.
  • Easily digested.
  • Contains antibodies and immune factors.
  • Reduces respiratory and gastrointestinal infections.
  • Supports normal growth and development.
  • Reduces the risk of childhood overweight and obesity.

4 benefitsFor the mother

  • Helps uterine contraction after delivery.
  • May reduce postpartum bleeding.
  • Promotes mother–infant bonding.
  • Convenient and economical.
Why it works

Suckling releases oxytocin, which ejects milk and contracts the uterus. In the long term, breastfeeding also lowers the mother’s risk of breast and ovarian cancer.

Colostrum: liquid gold

Colostrum is the thick, yellowish milk produced during the first few days after birth.

  • Rich in antibodies, especially IgA.
  • Rich in proteins and immune cells.
  • Easy to digest.
  • Provides protection against infection.
  • Helps establish the infant’s gastrointestinal tract.
Important

Colostrum should never be discarded.

Breastfeeding technique

The nurse teaches the mother to:

  1. Wash hands.
  2. Choose a comfortable position.
  3. Keep the baby’s head and body in a straight line.
  4. Keep the baby close to the mother.
  5. Wait until the baby’s mouth opens widely.
  6. Baby takes the nipple and much of the areola into the mouth.
  7. Observe effective sucking and swallowing.
  8. Let the baby finish one breast before offering the other.
The goal

Good latch → less nipple pain + effective milk transfer

Clinical pearl

Nose opposite the nipple, wait for the wide gape, then bring the baby to the breast, not the breast to the baby.

Signs of effective breastfeeding

Feeding frequency & burping

  • Feed on demand, according to hunger cues.
  • Most newborns breastfeed about 8–12 times / 24 h.
  • Avoid rigid feeding schedules in the early neonatal period.

Hunger cues

Early“I’m hungry”Rooting · Lip smacking
Active“I’m really hungry”Hand-to-mouth movements · Restlessness
Late“Calm me first”Crying is a late cue: soothe the baby, then feed.
feed nowtime passing
After feeding

Hold the baby upright. Burping helps, especially if the baby swallowed air: over the shoulder, sitting on the lap, or across the lap.

Artificial feeding

Artificial feeding means feeding an infant with infant formula instead of breast milk, either partially or completely.

It may be used when

  • Breastfeeding is contraindicated.
  • Breast milk is unavailable or insufficient.
  • The mother chooses formula feeding.
  • Certain medical conditions require alternative feeding.
Important

Breast milk is preferred whenever it is safe and available.

Examples of contraindications

Classic galactosaemia in the infant · some maternal medicines (e.g., chemotherapy) · certain maternal infections, according to national guidance.

Nursing attitude

Support the family’s feeding choice without judgement, and teach them to formula-feed safely.

Types of infant formula

Most commonStandard cow’s-milk-based

For most healthy term infants.

Modified cow’s milk protein, adjusted to resemble breast milk.

Premature babiesPreterm formula

Designed for premature infants, given according to medical advice.

Higher energy, protein and minerals to support catch-up growth.

Specific conditionsSpecialised formulas

  • Cow’s milk protein allergy (extensively hydrolysed or amino-acid based)
  • Certain metabolic disorders (e.g., PKU)
  • Malabsorption
Rule

Formula is selected according to the infant’s needs and healthcare advice, not by price or advertising.

Safe formula preparation

The nurse teaches the caregiver, in this order:

  1. Wash hands.
  2. Clean (and sterilise) feeding equipment.
  3. Use safe drinking water.
  4. Follow the manufacturer’s exact water-to-powder ratio.
  5. Measure water and formula accurately: water first, then level scoops.
  6. Prepare feeds safely; check the temperature on the inner wrist.
  7. Store feeds according to instructions.
  8. Discard leftover formula after feeding.
Never

Add extra powder · dilute excessively

Incorrect dilution can cause serious complications.

Clinical pearl · WHO

Powdered formula is not sterile. Make it up with water at no less than 70 °C (boiled, then cooled for no more than 30 minutes), cool it quickly, and discard any feed not used within 2 hours.

Dilution simulator: what does one scoop change?

Correct strength

Bottle-feeding technique

Do

  • Hold the infant in a semi-upright position.
  • Support the baby’s head and neck.
  • Tilt the bottle so the nipple stays filled with milk.
  • Allow the baby to pause during feeding.
  • Burp the baby when appropriate.

Never

  • Force the infant to finish the bottle.
  • Prop the bottle and leave the infant alone.
  • Feed an infant lying completely flat.

Propping risks choking and aspiration, ear infections and tooth decay, and the carer misses the baby’s fullness cues.

Clinical pearl · paced bottle feeding

Hold the bottle more horizontally, let the baby draw the teat in, and pause every few sucks. It mimics breastfeeding and helps prevent overfeeding.

Complications of artificial feeding

VolumeToo much, too fast

  • Overfeeding
  • Vomiting or regurgitation

GutDigestive problems

  • Diarrhoea
  • Constipation
  • Feeding intolerance

HygieneInfection

  • From contaminated bottles or formula

Preparation · most dangerousIncorrect formula preparation

Wrong dilution → dehydration or electrolyte disturbances (high or low sodium).

Long termTeeth

  • Dental caries with inappropriate, prolonged bottle use (e.g., bottle at bedtime)

What must you confirm before every single NG feed?

NG tube feeding

Nasogastric (NG) feeding is giving nutrition, fluids or medication through a tube passed through the nose into the stomach.

It may be used when the infant or child

  • Cannot feed safely by mouth.
  • Has inadequate oral intake.
  • Has impaired swallowing.
  • Has certain neurological or medical conditions.
  • Requires temporary enteral nutrition.
Safety

NG feeding requires trained healthcare personnel and appropriate tube-placement verification.

Indications for NG feeding

  • Premature or medically unstable infants with inadequate oral feeding.
  • Neurological disorders affecting swallowing.
  • Reduced level of consciousness, when enteral feeding is appropriate.
  • Severe weakness or fatigue during oral feeding.
  • Conditions causing inadequate oral intake.
Prerequisite

The gastrointestinal tract must be functional for enteral feeding.

Choosing the feeding route

Yes

No

Yes

Golden rule

Never give a feed if tube placement is uncertain.

Clinical pearl

Common confirmation methods are pH of aspirate and X-ray, per policy. Listening for a “whoosh” of air is not reliable: it can sound the same when the tube is in the lung.

NG feeding procedure

  1. Perform hand hygiene.
  2. Position the infant safely, usually semi-upright.
  3. Verify NG tube placement according to protocol.
  4. Check the prescribed feed.
  5. Give the feed at the prescribed rate.
  6. Observe the infant throughout the feed.
  7. Stop if concerning signs occur (see below).
  8. Flush the tube only according to the prescribed protocol.
  9. Keep the infant appropriately positioned after the feed.
  10. Document the feed and the infant’s response.
STOP

Stop the feed for significant respiratory distress, vomiting, coughing, cyanosis or any other concerning sign.

Clinical pearls

Bolus feeds usually run by gravity; do not push them with the plunger. Keep the head raised about 30–45° during and after the feed.

Complications of NG feeding

RespiratoryBreathing

  • Aspiration
  • Coughing
  • Respiratory distress
  • Cyanosis

GastrointestinalGut

  • Vomiting
  • Abdominal distension
  • Diarrhoea
  • Constipation
  • Feeding intolerance

Tube-relatedTube

  • Nasal irritation
  • Pressure injury
  • Tube displacement
  • Tube blockage
Most serious complication

Aspiration: feed entering the airway and lungs.

Watch for coughing, choking, colour change, falling oxygen saturation, fast breathing or distress, and stop the feed at the first sign.

Keep this question in mind

Why is 6 months the turning point?

Weaning = complementary feeding

Weaning here means introducing complementary foods while continuing breastfeeding, starting at about 6 months of age.

Why at 6 months?

  • Breast milk alone no longer provides all nutritional requirements.
  • The infant needs additional energy and nutrients, especially iron.
  • It supports the development of chewing and swallowing skills.

Texture grows with the child

0–6 mBreast milk only
≈ 6 mSmooth purées, mashed foods
≈ 8 mLumpier foods, soft finger foods
12 mFamily foods, chopped
Remember

Breastfeeding continues alongside complementary feeding.

Principles of weaning

Start around 6 monthsWhen breast milk alone is no longer enough.
Begin with small amountsA few spoonfuls, then increase.
Introduce foods graduallyOne new food at a time makes reactions easier to spot.
Increase variety & textureAs the child develops.
Continue breastfeedingIt stays a key source of nutrition and protection.
Use nutrient-rich foodsIron, protein, energy, vitamins and minerals.
Encourage responsive feedingFollow hunger and fullness cues; feed slowly and patiently.
Maintain good hygieneClean hands, utensils and safe food storage.
Do not force the child to eatPressure makes mealtimes stressful and can reduce intake.

Clinical pearl · iron first

The iron stores a baby is born with run low at around 6 months. Offer iron-rich foods early, with a vitamin C food (fruit, tomato) to boost absorption.

Foods to avoid

  • HHard, round foodsWhole nuts, whole grapes: choking risk unless prepared safely
  • HHigh salt or added sugarStrain immature kidneys; harm teeth and habits
  • UUnpasteurised milk & productsRisk of serious infection
  • UUnsafe or contaminated foodFood-borne diarrhoeal illness
Memory hook

3 H + 2 U

Honey · Hard round foods · High salt/sugar · Unpasteurised · Unsafe

And one more

Cow’s milk is not the main drink before 12 months.

It is low in iron. A little can be used in cooking.

Make it safe

Cut grapes and cherry tomatoes lengthwise into quarters. Offer nuts ground or as smooth nut butter, never whole.

      Case studies: what would you do?

      Day 3 · breastfeeding

      Amal’s baby feeds often, but Amal has cracked, painful nipples. You see the baby’s mouth only on the nipple, dimpled cheeks, and you hear clicking sounds.

      What is the problem? What will you teach?

      Problem

      Shallow (poor) latch

      Nursing action

      Reposition: head and body in line, baby close, nose opposite the nipple, wait for a wide-open mouth, bring baby to breast. Check CALMS. Continue breastfeeding; monitor wet nappies and weight; refer for lactation support if pain persists.

      2 months · formula

      A father adds an extra scoop to each bottle “so the baby sleeps longer”. The baby is irritable, has hard stools and fewer wet nappies.

      What is the risk? How do you respond?

      Problem

      Over-concentrated formula

      Risk of hypernatraemic dehydration and constipation.

      Nursing action

      Assess hydration (wet nappies, fontanelle, mucous membranes) and report. Teach the exact label ratio: water first, then level scoops, never extra powder. Confirm understanding with teach-back.

      Preterm · NG feeding

      A 34-week preterm infant is on bolus NG feeds. Halfway through a feed, the infant coughs, desaturates and becomes cyanosed.

      What do you suspect? What are your actions, in order?

      Suspect

      Aspiration

      Nursing action

      Stop the feed immediately. Support the airway (position, suction per protocol), give oxygen as ordered, call for help and assess. Do not restart until the team reviews and tube placement is re-confirmed. Document and report.

      7 months · weaning

      A mother gives her 7-month-old honey in warm water “for his throat”, and whole grapes as a snack while he plays alone on the floor.

      How many hazards can you find?

      3 hazards

      Honey before 12 months (botulism) · whole grapes (choking) · eating unsupervised and not seated.

      Nursing action

      No honey until 12 months. Cut grapes lengthwise into quarters. Feed seated upright and supervised. Offer iron-rich mashed foods and continue breastfeeding.
