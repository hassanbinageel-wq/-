What is breastfeeding?

Breastfeeding is feeding an infant with breast milk directly from the mother’s breast.

First 6 months

Exclusive breastfeeding

  • Breast milk is the only food and drink.
  • Recommended for the first 6 months of life.
  • No water, juice or other foods are routinely needed.
6 months

After 6 months

Breastfeeding + complementary foods

+
  • Continue breastfeeding.
  • Start complementary foods.

Benefits of breastfeeding

For the infant

  • Provides ideal nutrition.
  • Easily digested.
  • Contains antibodies and immune factors.
  • Reduces respiratory and gastrointestinal infections.
  • Supports normal growth and development.
  • Reduces risk of childhood overweight and obesity.

For the mother

  • Helps uterine contraction after delivery.
  • May reduce postpartum bleeding.
  • Promotes mother–infant bonding.
  • Convenient and economical.

Colostrum

Colostrum is the thick, yellowish milk produced during the first few days after birth.

  • Rich in antibodies, especially IgA.
  • Rich in proteins and immune cells.
  • Easy to digest.
  • Provides protection against infection.
  • Helps establish the infant’s gastrointestinal tract.
Important

Colostrum should not be discarded.

Breastfeeding technique

The nurse should teach the mother:

  1. Wash hands.
  2. Choose a comfortable position.
  3. Keep the baby’s head and body in a straight line.
  4. Keep the baby close to the mother.
  5. Baby’s mouth should open widely.
  6. Baby should take the nipple and much of the areola into the mouth.
  7. Observe effective sucking and swallowing.
  8. Allow the baby to finish one breast before offering the other.
Wide-open mouthNipple + much of the areola

Good latch → less nipple pain + effective milk transfer.

Signs of effective breastfeeding

The nurse should observe

  • Baby has a wide-open mouth.
  • Chin touches the breast.
  • More areola visible above the baby’s mouth than below.
  • Slow, deep sucking.
  • Audible swallowing.
  • Baby appears satisfied after feeding.
  • Adequate urine output and weight gain.

Poor latch may cause

Nipple pain·Cracked nipples·Poor milk transfer·Inadequate infant weight gain

Breastfeeding frequency & burping

  • Feed the newborn on demand, according to hunger cues.
  • Most newborns breastfeed approximately 8–12 times / 24 hours.
  • Avoid rigid feeding schedules in the early neonatal period.

After feeding

Hold the baby upright. Burping may be helpful, especially if the baby swallowed air.

What happens if formula is prepared incorrectly?

Artificial feeding

Artificial feeding means feeding an infant with infant formula instead of breast milk, either partially or completely.

It may be used when:

Breastfeeding is contraindicated.
Breast milk is unavailable or insufficient.
The mother chooses formula feeding.
Certain medical conditions require alternative feeding.
Important

Breast milk is preferred whenever it is safe and available.

Types of infant formula

1 · Standard cow’s-milk-based

Suitable for most healthy term infants.

2 · Preterm formula

Designed for premature infants according to medical advice.

3 · Specialised formulas

  • Cow’s milk protein allergy
  • Certain metabolic disorders
  • Malabsorption
Rule

Formula should be selected according to the infant’s needs and healthcare advice.

Safe formula preparation

The nurse should teach the caregiver:

Wash hands.1
Clean feeding equipment.2
Use safe drinking water.3
Follow the manufacturer’s exact water-to-powder ratio.4
Measure water and formula accurately.5
Do not add extra powder.6
Do not dilute formula excessively.7
Prepare feeds safely and store them according to instructions.8
Discard leftover formula after feeding.9

Incorrect dilution can cause serious complications.

Correct

Bottle-feeding technique

Do

  • Hold the infant in a semi-upright position.
  • Support the baby’s head and neck.
  • Keep the bottle tilted so the nipple remains filled with milk.
  • Allow the baby to pause during feeding.
  • Burp the baby when appropriate.

Never

  • Force the infant to finish the bottle.
  • Prop the bottle and leave the infant alone.
  • Feed an infant while lying completely flat.

Nipple filled with milk ✓

Keep the bottle tilted so the nipple remains filled with milk.

Complications of artificial feeding

Overfeeding.
Vomiting or regurgitation.
Diarrhoea.
Constipation.
Feeding intolerance.
Infection from contaminated bottles or formula.
Incorrect formula preparation → dehydration or electrolyte disturbances.
Increased risk of dental caries with inappropriate prolonged bottle use.

NG tube feeding

Nasogastric (NG) feeding is the administration of nutrition, fluids or medication through a tube inserted through the nose into the stomach.

It may be used when the infant / child:

  • Cannot feed safely by mouth.
  • Has inadequate oral intake.
  • Has impaired swallowing.
  • Has certain neurological or medical conditions.
  • Requires temporary enteral nutrition.
Safety

NG feeding requires trained healthcare personnel and appropriate tube-placement verification.

Nose Oesophagus Stomach Airway

Indications for NG feeding

NG feeding may be indicated in:

Premature or medically unstable infants with inadequate oral feeding.
Neurological disorders affecting swallowing.
Reduced level of consciousness, when enteral feeding is appropriate.
Severe weakness or fatigue during oral feeding.
Conditions causing inadequate oral intake.
Requirement

The gastrointestinal tract should be functional for enteral feeding.

Before NG feeding

The nurse should — tick each check:

Tick all 7 checks to unlock the feed.

Never administer a feed if tube placement is uncertain.

NG feeding procedure

  1. Perform hand hygiene.
  2. Position the infant safely, usually semi-upright.
  3. Verify NG tube placement according to protocol.
  4. Check the prescribed feed.
  5. Administer the feed at the prescribed rate.
  6. Observe the infant throughout feeding.
  7. Stop feeding if concerning signs occur (see below).
  8. Flush the tube only according to the prescribed / appropriate protocol.
  9. Keep the infant appropriately positioned after feeding.
  10. Document the feeding and the infant’s response.
STOP

Stop feeding if significant respiratory distress, vomiting, coughing, cyanosis or other concerning signs occur.

Respiratory

Aspiration · Coughing · Respiratory distress · Cyanosis

Gastrointestinal

Vomiting · Abdominal distension · Diarrhoea · Constipation · Feeding intolerance

Tube-related

Nasal irritation · Pressure injury · Tube displacement or blockage

Most serious complication

Aspiration.

Why do complementary foods start at about 6 months?

Weaning / complementary feeding

Weaning in this context means introducing complementary foods while continuing breastfeeding.

Why at about 6 months?

  • Breast milk alone no longer provides all nutritional requirements.
  • The infant needs additional energy and nutrients.
  • It supports development of chewing and swallowing skills.
6months
Remember

Breastfeeding should continue alongside complementary feeding.

Principles of weaning

When introducing complementary foods:

Start around 6 months.
Begin with small amounts.
Introduce foods gradually.
Increase variety and texture as the child develops.
Continue breastfeeding.
Use nutrient-rich foods.
Encourage responsive feeding.
Maintain good hygiene.
Do not force the child to eat.

Foods during weaning

Offer a variety of:
