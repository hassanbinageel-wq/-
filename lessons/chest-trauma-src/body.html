What is trauma?

Trauma is a physical injury caused by an external force that damages body tissues or organs.

Common causes of trauma

Road traffic accidents
Falls from heights
Stab wounds
Gunshot wounds
Burns
Physical assault

Types of trauma

Blunt trauma

Injury without penetration of the skin.

Penetrating trauma

Injury caused by an object entering the body.

Chest trauma is an injury to the chest wall or internal organs of the chest.

Why is it dangerous?

Chest trauma can cause:

1

Respiratory failure

2

Severe bleeding

3

Shock

4

Death if not treated quickly

Classification of chest trauma

1

Blunt chest trauma

skin not penetrated
  • Caused by road traffic accidents.
  • Falls.
  • Direct blows to the chest.
  • May cause rib fractures or lung injuries.
2

Penetrating chest trauma

object enters the chest
  • Caused by knives or bullets.
  • May damage the lungs, heart, or blood vessels.
  • Can cause severe internal bleeding.

Clinical assessment · the ABCDE approach

The initial assessment follows the ABCDE approach.

A

Airway

Check if the airway is open.

B

Breathing

Assess respiratory rate, chest movement and oxygen saturation.

C

Circulation

Check pulse, blood pressure and signs of bleeding.

D

Disability

Assess consciousness and neurological status.

E

Exposure

Examine the whole body for injuries while preventing hypothermia.

Important: Life-threatening problems must be treated immediately.

Chest wall injuries · rib fracture

Fracture Drag to rotate

Definition

A rib fracture is a break in one or more ribs, usually caused by direct trauma to the chest.

Clinical manifestations

  • Severe chest pain.
  • Pain increases with deep breathing or coughing.
  • Tenderness over the fractured rib.
  • Sometimes crepitus.

Rib fracture · complications & management

Complications

Lung injury

Management

Pain control
Oxygen if needed
Encourage deep breathing and coughing
Monitor respiratory status
Note: Chest binding is not recommended because it restricts breathing.

Definition

Flail chest occurs when at least three consecutive ribs are each fractured in two or more places, creating a free-floating segment of the chest wall.

Characteristic sign: paradoxical movement

During inspirationThe injured segment moves inward.
During expirationThe injured segment moves outward.

Flail chest · manifestations & management

Clinical manifestations

Severe chest pain
Respiratory distress
Rapid breathing
Low oxygen saturation

Management

  1. Oxygen therapy.
  2. Adequate pain control.
  3. Respiratory support.
  4. Mechanical ventilation in severe cases.

Pleural injuries · pneumothorax

Pneumothorax is the presence of air in the pleural space (pleural cavity).

Classification

Spontaneous pneumothorax

Primary

  • No evidence of overt lung disease.
  • Occurs in patients with no history of lung disease.

Secondary

  • Underlying lung disease is present.

Clinical picture of spontaneous pneumothorax

History of the cause (in secondary).

Sudden-onset unilateral pleuritic chest pain and breathlessness.

With underlying lung disease: breathlessness can be severe and may not resolve spontaneously.

Traumatic pneumothorax · 3 types

Normal

The lung fills the pleural cavity. Choose a type above to see what changes.

Type 1

Closed (simple) pneumothorax

  • Limited amount of air is entrapped into the pleural cavity.
  • Not communicating with the exterior.
Type 2

Open pneumothorax

Definition

  • The pleural space communicates freely with the exterior.

Pathology · paradoxical respiration

  • Collapses more during inspiration.
  • Expands slightly during expiration.
Type 3

Tension pneumothorax

Definition

  • Valvular tear which allows air to enter but does not allow it to come out from the pleural space.

Pathology

  • Respiratory failure due to mediastinal shift to the opposite side, affecting the normal lung.
  • Shock due to mediastinal shift affecting the heart.

Clinical picture of traumatic pneumothorax

History of trauma.
Symptoms: acute chest pain, dyspnea, cough.

Signs · general

Shock
Distended neck veins
Cyanosis
Respiratory distress

Signs · local

  • Decreased chest movement on the affected side.
  • Shifting of the trachea to the opposite side Tension pneumothorax
  • Decreased air entry.

The clinical picture of tension pneumothorax is similar to open but differs in:

More severe cardiorespiratory distressPresence of mediastinal shift

  • Once tension pneumothorax is clinically diagnosed, a wide-bore needle is immediately placed in the second intercostal space in the midclavicular line.
